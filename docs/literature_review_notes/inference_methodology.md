## 1. Replication failures of architecture claims, and what caused them

### Takeaway
In every well-known re-evaluation (LSTMs vs. NAS cells, GANs, Transformer modifications, metric learning, NAS, efficient-training methods), most of the claimed architectural gain disappeared once baselines were tuned equally, implementations were shared, and seed variance was counted. The recurring causes were: unequal tuning budgets, codebase and implementation confounds, weak or mis-specified baselines, test-set feedback, reporting the best run, and metrics that could not separate methods.

### Cited Findings

**Melis, Dyer, Blunsom: "On the State of the Art of Evaluation in Neural Language Models" (2017 arXiv; ICLR 2018, venue: background knowledge)** — https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.05589
- Earlier RNN results came from different codebases and limited compute, which the authors call "uncontrolled sources of experimental variation." They re-tuned everything with large-scale black-box tuning (Google Vizier) and found "standard LSTM architectures, when properly regularised, outperform more recent models." — [arXiv abs](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.05589)
- PTB test perplexity, 10M params: LSTM 59.6, NAS 62.7, RHN 63.5. At 24M: LSTM 58.3, NAS 59.7, RHN 62.2. Wikitext-2 at 24M: LSTM 65.9, NAS 69.8, RHN 75.6. The original 24M Zaremba LSTM scored 78.4 on PTB, so tuning alone was worth about 20 perplexity points. — [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1707.05589)
- Tuning cost: about 1,500 tuner trials for the 4-layer LSTM. A grid search with 5 values per hyperparameter would need nearly 8,000 trials to get within 3.0 perplexity of that result. The authors say "thousands of evaluations are required to reach convergence." — [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1707.05589)
- Noise floor: retraining with different seeds (and nondeterminism) gives about 0.4 perplexity of variation on PTB and 0.5 on Wikitext-2. The validation-to-test gap adds about 0.12–0.3. The authors treat 1.0 perplexity as a statistically robust difference. — [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1707.05589)

**Lucic, Kurach, Michalski, Gelly, Bousquet: "Are GANs Created Equal? A Large-Scale Study" (2017 arXiv; NeurIPS 2018, venue: background knowledge)** — https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.10337
- Compared 7 GAN variants plus a VAE baseline on 4 datasets, with 100 random hyperparameter samples per model (wide search) and 50 (narrow). The study took about 6.85 P100 GPU-years. — [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1711.10337v4)
- Finding: "most models can reach similar scores with enough hyperparameter optimization and random restarts." Gains may come from "higher computational budget and tuning more than fundamental algorithmic changes." No algorithm consistently beat the original non-saturating GAN (Goodfellow 2014). — [arXiv abs](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.10337)
- Seed variance of the best configuration rerun 50 times, e.g. CIFAR-10 FID: WGAN 55.2±2.3, WGAN-GP 55.8±0.9, LSGAN 87.1±47.5 (outlier runs). The ranking depends on compute budget, and the authors say it is "meaningless to report the minimum FID achieved." They recommend reporting the distribution of FID under a fixed budget. — [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1711.10337v4)

**Narang et al.: "Do Transformer Modifications Transfer Across Implementations and Applications?" (2021 arXiv; EMNLP 2021, venue: background knowledge)** — https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.11972
- "Surprisingly, we find that most modifications do not meaningfully improve performance." Most variants that did help were developed in the same codebase the authors used, or were minor changes. The authors conjecture that gains "may strongly depend on implementation details." — [arXiv abs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.11972)
- Table 1 holds about 50 variant rows (activations, normalization, depth, embeddings, parameter sharing, softmax, architectures). The ones that helped: GLU-family activations (SwiGLU, GeGLU), RMSNorm, more depth at fixed parameters (but slower), untied embeddings, mixture-of-softmaxes (~40% slower), two Synthesizer variants, and Switch/MoE/product-key memory (which add parameters). ReZero, Fixup and parameter sharing hurt. — [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2102.11972v2)
- Protocol: hyperparameters were held fixed across variants. Each variant ran 5 times for 65,536 steps, and the paper reports mean ± std. In a Universal Transformer case study the authors swept 25 configurations. Only 2 beat the initial result, and the best (loss 2.265) still trailed the vanilla Transformer (2.182). This is evidence that the fixed-hyperparameter design did not unfairly sink that variant. — [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2102.11972v2)
- Recommendations: test in multiple disparate codebases and across many downstream applications; keep hyperparameters fixed or report robustness to them; report mean and std over multiple trials; avoid cherry-picking. — [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2102.11972v2)
- Note: the exact "X of Y" count was not stated in the abstract, and I did not verify it in the PDF table. The figure "~50 variants, roughly a dozen helped, mostly cheap or same-codebase" is my reading of the fetched summary.

**Musgrave, Belongie, Lim: "A Metric Learning Reality Check" (2020 arXiv; ECCV 2020, venue: background knowledge)** — https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.08505
- Flaws found: unequal backbones (ResNet50 vs. GoogleNet). Before any metric-learning training, ResNet50 already beats GoogleNet on Recall@1: 48.7 vs. 41.1 (CUB200), 43.5 vs. 33.9 (Cars196), 52.9 vs. 45.2 (SOP). Other flaws: unequal embedding sizes, undisclosed augmentation, undisclosed frozen BatchNorm (~2-point boost on CUB200), no confidence intervals, and hyperparameters tuned with test-set feedback because there was no validation set. — [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2003.08505v3)
- Metric artifacts: Recall@1 is near 100% for toy embeddings with very different separation. NMI inflates scores for many-class datasets: a random network scores about 81 NMI on SOP but about 19–24 on CUB/Cars. — [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2003.08505v3)
- Papers claimed relative improvements above 100% over contrastive loss. Under a fair protocol (10 runs, 128-dim), the 2006–2019 trend was roughly flat. Examples: CUB200 P@1, contrastive 59.73 vs. best 60.17. SOP P@1, contrastive 69.34 vs. best 71.65. "The actual improvements over time have been marginal at best." — [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2003.08505v3); [abs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.08505)

**Li & Talwalkar: "Random Search and Reproducibility for Neural Architecture Search" (2019 arXiv; UAI 2019, venue: background knowledge)** — https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.07638
- Random search with early stopping performs at least as well as ENAS on PTB and CIFAR-10. Random search with weight sharing does better still and reached a NAS state of the art on PTB. The authors document missing material needed to reproduce published NAS results and sensitivity to variability in experimental setups. — [arXiv abs](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.07638)

**Kaddour, Key, Nawrot, Minervini, Kusner: "No Train No Gain" (NeurIPS 2023)** — https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/hash/51f3d6252706100325ddc435ba0ade0e-Abstract.html
- Tested dynamic architectures (layer stacking, layer dropping), batch selection (selective backprop, RHO loss) and optimizers (Lion, Sophia) on BERT and T5 under a fixed "reference system time" budget. Against a baseline with a fully decayed learning rate, the training, validation and downstream gains "vanish." — [arXiv abs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.06440v1)

**Henderson et al.: "Deep Reinforcement Learning that Matters" (AAAI 2018)** — https://arxiv.org/abs/1709.06560
- TRPO on HalfCheetah: 10 runs with identical hyperparameters, split into two groups of 5 seeds. The group mean curves differed significantly (t = −9.0916, p = 0.0016). Seed groups alone can produce "significant" differences. — [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1709.06560v3)
- Network architecture interacts with other hyperparameters: PPO with a (400,300) network had about 33.5× higher update KL divergence than with (64,64), so the clipping or learning rate would need retuning. Averaging N<5 runs or reporting top runs is misleading. — [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1709.06560v3)

**Training recipe as a confound for architecture comparisons**
- Wightman, Touvron, Jégou, "ResNet strikes back" (2021 arXiv; NeurIPS 2021 workshop, venue: background knowledge): with a modern training recipe, a vanilla ResNet-50 reaches 80.4% ImageNet top-1 at 224px, with no extra data or distillation. ResNets serve as baselines for new architectures, so an old recipe understates the baseline. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.00476)
- Bello et al., "Revisiting ResNets" (2021 arXiv; NeurIPS 2021, venue: background knowledge): "training and scaling strategies may matter more than architectural changes." Re-trained ResNet-RS models are 1.7–2.7× faster than EfficientNets on TPUs at similar accuracy. The best scaling strategy (depth vs. width) depends on the training regime. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.07579)
- Schmidt, Schneider, Hennig, "Descending through a Crowded Valley" (2020 arXiv; ICML 2021, venue: background knowledge): 15 optimizers, more than 50,000 runs. No optimizer dominates. Trying several optimizers at default settings is about as good as tuning one. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.01547)

**Scaling-law replication: Besiroglu, Erdil, Barnett, You: "Chinchilla Scaling: A replication attempt" (2024 arXiv)** — https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.10102
- Hoffmann et al.'s Approach 3 (parametric fit) disagreed with their Approaches 1 and 2. It implied about 70 tokens per parameter instead of 20. Refitting data extracted from the original plots gives about 20 tokens per parameter, consistent with Approaches 1 and 2. — [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.10102)
- Cause: Hoffmann averaged the Huber loss instead of summing it, and "the high loss scale of their optimizer caused their optimization to terminate early." Rounding β to 0.28 alone causes about 13% bias at D=10^11. Revised estimates: E=1.817, A=482, B=2085, α=0.348, β=0.366, versus Hoffmann's E=1.69, A=406.4, B=410.7, α=0.34, β=0.28. — [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.10102)
- The reported confidence intervals (width ~0.001 for a) would need about 600,000 runs. Hoffmann likely had 400–500. The revised standard error for a is 0.018. Even with unrounded values, a likelihood-ratio test rejects the original fit at p ≈ 1.2×10^-16. — [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.10102)
- Porian, Wortsman, Jitsev, Schmidt, Carmon, "Resolving Discrepancies in Compute-Optimal Scaling of Language Models" (2024 arXiv; NeurIPS 2024, venue: background knowledge): the Kaplan vs. Hoffmann gap comes from three factors: last-layer compute accounting, warmup duration, and scale-dependent optimizer tuning. Correcting them reproduces Hoffmann. Careful LR decay was not essential, and AdamW β2 tuning matters at small batch sizes. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.19146)

**Measurement artifacts producing apparent "emergence"**
- Schaeffer, Miranda, Koyejo, "Are Emergent Abilities of Large Language Models a Mirage?" (2023 arXiv; NeurIPS 2023, venue: background knowledge): applying different metrics to the same model outputs can create or remove apparent emergence. Nonlinear or discontinuous metrics (e.g. exact match) produce sharp jumps, and continuous metrics show smooth change. The authors also induced "emergence" in vision networks through metric choice alone. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.15004)

**Meta-critiques**
- Lipton & Steinhardt, "Troubling Trends in Machine Learning Scholarship" (2018 arXiv; ICML 2018 debates workshop / later ACM Queue, venue: background knowledge). Four trends: (1) failing to separate explanation from speculation; (2) failing to identify the sources of empirical gains, e.g. crediting architecture for gains that came from tuning; (3) mathiness; (4) misuse of language. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.03341)
- Sculley, Snoek, Rahimi, Wiltschko, "Winner's Curse? On Pace, Progress, and Empirical Rigor" (ICLR 2018 Workshop): the culture rewards "wins," not knowledge. Many parallel researchers on shared benchmarks create multiple-hypothesis-testing problems. Recommendations: tune all key hyperparameters for every model including baselines; sliced analysis; ablations in isolation and in combination; sanity checks and counterfactual / OOD evaluation; report negative results; keep time-stamped experiment logs to limit post-hoc stories. — [OpenReview PDF](https://openreview.net/pdf?id=rJWF0Fywf)

### Inferences
- Recurring failure modes that apply directly to topology experiments: (a) the new routing gets more tuning attention than the baseline (Melis, Lucic, Musgrave); (b) the effect lives in implementation details the paper attributes to the architecture (Narang, Musgrave's frozen BN); (c) the baseline schedule is under-trained (Kaddour's fully-decayed LR baseline; ResNet strikes back); (d) the metric creates the effect (Schaeffer, Musgrave's NMI); (e) the fitting procedure or its confidence intervals are wrong (Besiroglu).
- Size of the noise floor: architecture effects claimed at about 1 seed-std (e.g. 0.2–0.5% accuracy, 0.4 perplexity) are inside the range where re-evaluations routinely erase them. A structural effect should be a large multiple of between-seed variation, measured under a fair tuning protocol.
- Residual vs. parallel vs. composed routing will differ in effective depth and signal propagation. So the optimal learning rate, warmup and init scale are likely to differ by topology. Henderson's KL example and Narang's ReZero/Fixup optimizer exception show architecture–hyperparameter interactions large enough to flip conclusions.

### Gaps
- The exact "X of Y modifications transferred" count from Narang et al. was not verified against the PDF table. The fetched summary lists about 50 rows and names roughly a dozen helpful variants.
- Venue for Lucic, Musgrave, Li & Talwalkar and Lipton & Steinhardt comes from background knowledge. The fetched pages showed only arXiv listings.
- I found no published replication study aimed specifically at "topology/routing changes emergent representation structure" claims. The cited failures are accuracy/loss claims. Structure claims are probably more fragile because the metrics are less standardized (see Q6).

## 2. Fair hyperparameter tuning across architectures

### Takeaway
Treat architecture as the "scientific" variable and optimizer/regularization settings as "nuisance" variables that must be optimized separately for each architecture. Report expected best performance as a function of tuning budget, randomize the HPO procedure itself, and use parametrizations such as μP / depth-μP or learning-rate-sensitivity curves so that a "win" does not depend on one hand-picked learning rate.

### Cited Findings
- **Google Deep Learning Tuning Playbook (Godbole, Dahl, Gilmer, Shallue, Nado; GitHub, 2023):** "Scientific hyperparameters are those whose effect on the model's performance we're trying to measure." "Nuisance hyperparameters are those that need to be optimized over in order to fairly compare" scientific settings. Fixed hyperparameters add caveats to conclusions. Example: "we can only fairly compare models with different numbers of hidden layers if the learning rate is tuned separately" for each. The more a nuisance hyperparameter interacts with the scientific one, the more damaging it is to fix it. — [tuning_playbook](https://github.com/google-research/tuning_playbook)
- **Playbook, isolation plots:** "Each point on an isolation plot corresponds to the performance of the *best* trial" over the nuisance hyperparameters. Optimizing the nuisance settings away gives apples-to-apples comparisons, and "the baseline should also have its learning rate equally well tuned." — [tuning_playbook](https://github.com/google-research/tuning_playbook)
- **Playbook, variance:** distinguishes *trial variance* (same hyperparameters, different seeds) from *study variance* (from the hyperparameter search itself, e.g. quasi-random search seed) and says "we are most concerned about study variance when trying to make conclusions." It recommends rerunning the best trial N times; no N is given. Most effort should go to exploration and insight, not exploitation. — [tuning_playbook](https://github.com/google-research/tuning_playbook)
- **Dodge, Gururangan, Card, Schwartz, Smith, "Show Your Work: Improved Reporting of Experimental Results" (2019 arXiv; EMNLP 2019, venue: background knowledge):** report *expected validation performance* of the best model as a function of budget (number of trials or training time). The estimator is E[V*_n | n] = Σ_v v·(P̂(V≤v)^n − P̂(V<v)^n), using the empirical CDF of the trials already run, so it needs no new training. — [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.03004v1)
- Dodge et al. budget reversals: (a) on SST-5, logistic regression beats a CNN below about 10 trials and the CNN wins with more; (b) on SST, GloVe beats ELMo under about 2 hours, frozen ELMo wins at about 6h–1 day, and fine-tuned ELMo wins at about 10 days; (c) matching BiDAF's reported SQuAD EM took about 18 GPU-days (55 trials), which suggests substantial unreported tuning. "The best model is a function of the budget." — [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.03004v1)
- Dodge checklist: hyperparameter bounds, best configurations, number of trials, search method and selection criterion, expected validation performance vs. trials, validation score for each test result, compute infrastructure, and runtimes. — [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.03004v1)
- **Bouthillier et al., "Accounting for Variance in Machine Learning Benchmarks" (2021 arXiv; MLSys 2021, venue: background knowledge):** HPO is itself a variance source. Random search, noisy grid search and Bayesian optimization each add about as much variance as weight initialization. — [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.03098v1)
- **Melis et al. 2018:** reusing PTB-tuned settings on Wikitext-2 was clearly worse than re-tuning on Wikitext-2. Tuned settings do not transfer across datasets. — [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1707.05589)
- **Bell, Kampman, Dodge, Lawrence, "Modeling the Machine Learning Multiverse" (NeurIPS 2022):** in a VGG-16/CIFAR-10 test of "SGD beats Adam" (Wilson et al. 2017), learning rate explained almost all variance. SGD wins for LR from about 1e-3 to about 3, Adam wins outside that range, and Adam's ε barely matters. "Optimizer choice matters little if the learning rate is properly tuned." — [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.05985); [NeurIPS](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2022/hash/750337e1301941f81ae31a90e0a1c181-Abstract.html)
- **μP / μTransfer: Yang et al., "Tensor Programs V" (2022 arXiv; NeurIPS 2021, venue: background knowledge):** under Maximal Update Parametrization, many optimal hyperparameters stay stable as width grows. Transferring from 13M params beat published BERT-large results (tuning cost equal to one BERT-large pretraining). Transferring from 40M params beat published 6.7B GPT-3 results at about 7% of pretraining cost. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.03466)
- **Depth-μP: Yang, Yu, Zhu, Hayou, "Tensor Programs VI" (2023 arXiv; ICLR 2024, venue: background knowledge):** a unique optimal parametrization for residual nets with one-layer blocks, giving hyperparameter transfer across depth and maximizing "feature learning and feature diversity." For blocks with more than one layer (as in Transformers), the authors find "fundamental limitations in all possible infinite-depth limits." — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.02244)
- **LR-sensitivity curves: Wortsman et al., "Small-scale proxies for large-scale Transformer training instabilities" (2023 arXiv; ICLR 2024, venue: background knowledge):** final loss vs. learning rate across scales is a diagnostic. Warmup, weight decay and μParam reduce LR sensitivity, giving small models whose loss stays similar across orders of magnitude of LR. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.14322)
- **AlgoPerf: Dahl et al., "Benchmarking Neural Network Training Algorithms" (2023 arXiv):** the field cannot "reliably identify training algorithm improvements." Three named challenges: defining when training is complete and timing it precisely; sensitivity to workload details; and fair tuning. The proposed fix is time-to-result on fixed hardware, across multiple workloads and workload variants. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.07179)
- **Porian et al. 2024:** scale-dependent optimizer tuning (and warmup) was one of three factors behind the Kaplan–Chinchilla discrepancy. Tuning that is fair at one scale is not fair at another. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.19146)

### Inferences
- For residual vs. parallel vs. composed routing at a fixed operator set and parameter count, treat **learning rate, warmup, weight decay, init scale / residual-branch multiplier and output multiplier** as nuisance parameters to tune per topology. Report isolation plots: the best structural metric (and loss) for each topology after optimizing out the nuisances, plus the full LR sweep.
- Give every topology the **same** search budget and search algorithm. Report Dodge-style curves of expected outcome vs. number of trials for both loss and the structural metric, so a reader can see whether the ranking flips with budget.
- Parametrization matters especially for topology. Composed (serial) vs. parallel arrangements change effective depth, so under standard parametrization the optimal LR and the lazy-vs-rich regime (Q4) can shift with topology alone. A depth-μP-style residual multiplier, or an explicit sweep over branch multiplier, keeps "the topology changed the structure" from collapsing into "the topology changed the effective learning rate." Depth-μP's caveat for blocks with more than one layer suggests checking transfer empirically, not assuming it.
- A compact fairness protocol: (1) coarse log-LR sweep per topology (e.g. 7–9 points over 3 decades) at 2–3 seeds; (2) confirm the optimum is interior, not at the sweep edge; (3) run final comparisons at each topology's own optimum **and** at a shared LR. If the conclusions agree, the claim is robust; if not, report both.

### Gaps
- No source gives a definitive "number of trials per architecture" rule. Dodge's curves let you read off budget sensitivity rather than prescribe one.
- I found no μP/depth-μP result specifically for parallel-branch or mixed serial/parallel topologies. Whether transfer holds there is open.
- The remaining ~19k characters of the tuning playbook were not read. The playbook guidance above comes from the first 100k characters.

## 3. Seed variance and statistics

### Takeaway
Seeds alone can produce "significant" differences between identical configurations. Data sampling is usually a larger variance source than init. Use paired designs (shared seeds and splits), randomize all variance sources including HPO, report distributions with bootstrap intervals, and test with a probability-of-improvement criterion. Bouthillier et al. derive about 29 runs per arm to detect P(A>B) ≥ 0.75 at α=β=0.05. Small effects (P(A>B) < 0.6) need hundreds of runs.

### Cited Findings
- **Picard, "torch.manual_seed(3407) is all you need" (2021 arXiv):** ResNet9 on CIFAR-10, 10,000 seeds (short setup): mean 90.02%, std 0.23, range 89.01–90.83% (1.82-point best-to-worst gap). With 500 seeds (longer setup): mean 90.70%, std 0.20, range 90.14–91.41%. ImageNet, 50 seeds with pretrained backbones and a from-scratch head: about 0.4–0.5-point spread (e.g. supervised ResNet50 75.48±0.10, range 75.25–75.69). Even with small variance, it is easy to find outlier seeds. Recommendation: report mean, std, min and max over seeds, and vary splits. — [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2109.08203v2); [abs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.08203)
- **Bouthillier et al. 2021:** data sampling (bootstrap) is "the most important source of variance in all case studies." Weight init is generally under 50% of bootstrap variance, comparable to data order. The HPO method adds variance comparable to init. Dropout and numerical noise are smaller. — [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.03098v1)
- **Bouthillier recommendations:** (1) randomize as many sources as possible (init, data split/order, augmentation, ideally HPO). Their "FixHOptEst(k, All)" estimator randomizes everything except HPO at about the cost of init-only seeding. IdealEst(k=100) cost about 1,070 GPU-h versus about 21 GPU-h for FixHOptEst, a 51× cut. (2) Use P(A>B) with γ=0.75 and percentile-bootstrap CIs: conclude A>B only if CI_min > 0.5 (significant) and CI_max > γ (meaningful). (3) Use out-of-bootstrap resampling instead of one fixed test set. (4) **Pair** A and B on the same seeds and splits. (5) About **29 runs per algorithm** for γ=0.75, α=β=0.05 (Noether). Detecting P(A>B) < 0.6 needs more than 500 runs, and below 0.55 more than 700. — [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.03098v1)
- **Bouthillier simulated error rates** (k=50): single-point comparison has about 10% false positives and 75% false negatives. Comparing averages has under 5% false positives but about 90% false negatives. P(A>B) with γ=0.75 has about 5% false positives and 30% false negatives. — [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.03098v1)
- **Henderson et al. 2018:** identical TRPO configurations split by seed (5 vs. 5) gave significantly different curves (p=0.0016). Use significance tests (t-test, KS, bootstrap CIs), bootstrap power analysis, and avoid N<5 or top-k reporting. — [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1709.06560v3)
- **Agarwal, Schwarzer, Castro, Courville, Bellemare, "Deep RL at the Edge of the Statistical Precipice" (2021 arXiv; NeurIPS 2021, venue: background knowledge):** point estimates from few runs gave different conclusions from a proper analysis on Atari 100k, and re-examining ALE, Procgen and DMC revealed discrepancies with prior claims. Tools: interval estimates of aggregate performance, performance profiles, interquartile mean (IQM, robust with few runs), and the `rliable` library. "Reliable evaluation in the few run deep RL regime cannot ignore the uncertainty in results." — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.13264)
- **Lucic et al. 2018:** the best configuration rerun 50 times can have a large spread (LSGAN CIFAR-10 FID 87.1 ± 47.5). Report the distribution under a fixed budget, not the minimum. — [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1711.10337v4)
- **Melis et al. 2018:** seed noise of about 0.4–0.5 perplexity; 1.0 treated as robust. — [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1707.05589)
- **Multiverse analysis: Bell et al. (NeurIPS 2022):** adapted from Steegen et al. 2016 (psychology). Define the space of reasonable analysis choices, fit a Gaussian-process surrogate of the outcome over it, choose points by Bayesian experimental design (Sobol init, integrated variance reduction), test interactions with Bayes factors, and report main and total sensitivity effects. "A claim is robust if it holds across a region of the search space, not just at a single tuned configuration." Case study 2 (LR × batch size × 3 models × 3 datasets) found no consistent large-batch generalization gap once LR was accounted for. Limitations: single run per point, homoscedastic noise, about 1,138 GPU-h. The authors suggest pairing it with preregistration. — [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.05985)
- **Preregistration in ML:** the NeurIPS 2020 workshop "The pre-registration experiment: an alternative publication model for machine learning research" reviewed papers *without results*. Proposals described the experimental protocol, grounded in theory or prior evidence, and were judged on design quality. Results followed acceptance. Rationale: "beat the SOTA" incentives and unpublished negative results. — [NeurIPS 2020 workshop](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2020/workshop/16158). A second edition ran at NeurIPS 2021 — [NeurIPS 2021 workshop](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2021/workshop/21885). Proceedings appear in PMLR vol. 148 — [PMLR v148](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v148/) (the PMLR link to the 2020 workshop is from search results and was not opened).
- **Reproducibility checklist: Pineau et al., "Improving Reproducibility in ML Research (NeurIPS 2019 Reproducibility Program)" (2020 arXiv; JMLR 2021, venue: background knowledge):** code-submission policy, reproducibility challenge, and the ML Reproducibility Checklist at submission. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.12206)
- **Sculley et al. 2018:** many groups testing hypotheses on shared benchmarks create multiple-comparison problems. Keep time-stamped experiment records. — [OpenReview PDF](https://openreview.net/pdf?id=rJWF0Fywf)

### Inferences
- **Unit of analysis:** for an architecture → structure claim, the unit is the *training run* (seed), not the example or neuron. Structural metrics computed over many neurons or examples within one run yield tight within-run CIs that say nothing about run-to-run variability. Bootstrap over runs, or use a hierarchical model (runs nested in topology), and report both levels.
- **Paired design:** give each topology the same seed set for data order, init RNG stream (where shapes match) and data subsample. Analyze per-seed differences. Matched data order is cheap and directly targets the dominant variance source (data sampling) that Bouthillier identifies.
- **Seed count:** about 5 seeds is a weak floor (Henderson; Narang used 5). About 10 lets you report min/max/IQM meaningfully. About 29+ per arm if you want to claim a P(A>B) ≥ 0.75 effect at α=β=0.05 (Bouthillier). For toy-scale topology experiments this is affordable and should be the default for headline claims.
- **Effect sizes:** report standardized effect size (Cohen's d on per-seed paired differences, or P(A>B)) alongside means. Bouthillier's two-sided criterion (significant *and* meaningful) maps directly onto "topology causes a structural change of practical size."
- **Multiple comparisons:** sweeping many structural metrics × layers × checkpoints × topologies inflates false discoveries. Preregister one or two primary metrics and a primary checkpoint rule. Treat the rest as exploratory, with Holm or Benjamini–Hochberg correction (standard statistics; not sourced from the ML papers above).
- **Seed ensembles:** Picard's 10,000-seed distribution is a template. Before the main study, run a cheap seed-only study (e.g. 50–100 seeds of the baseline topology at small scale) to measure the null distribution of each structural metric. Then any topology effect can be expressed in units of seed-std.
- **Multiverse:** for each headline claim, define the analysis multiverse (LR, init scale, width, data size, weight decay, metric hyperparameters such as CKA kernel or probe regularization, checkpoint choice). Report the region where the claim holds, as in Bell et al.

### Gaps
- No ML-specific source found here gives formal guidance on multiple-comparison correction for representation-analysis studies. The BH/Holm advice is general statistics.
- No source was found that quantifies seed variance of *structural* metrics (e.g. CKA, modularity, superposition measures) as opposed to accuracy. This should be measured in the program itself. Ding et al. (Q6) note that metrics disagree on whether seed-only variants are similar.

## 4. "Science of deep learning" methodology: synthetic tasks, toy models, phase diagrams, causal frameworks, scaling-law pitfalls

### Takeaway
The strongest mechanistic claims come from controlled synthetic tasks where ground-truth structure is known, from mapping outcomes as phase diagrams over control parameters (data size, weight decay, init/output scale, sparsity) instead of single points, and from interventions (ablations in a known basis) instead of correlations. Lazy-vs-rich regime, set by init or output scale, is a first-order confound for any "emergent structure" claim. Scaling fits need correct loss functions, honest CIs, and consistent compute accounting.

### Cited Findings
- **Allen-Zhu, "Physics of Language Models: Part 4.1, Architecture Design and the Magic of Canon Layers" (NeurIPS 2025):** architecture comparisons at academic scale (about 1.3B params / 100B tokens) are often "dominated by noise and randomness." Benchmarks such as PIQA and HellaSwag showed high seed-to-seed variance and unreliable rankings, and all models failed 2-hop reasoning. The proposed alternative is a "synthetic playground" of tasks that each target one atomic capability: Depo (reasoning depth, k-hop), Brevo (reasoning breadth, DAG topological sort), Capo (knowledge capacity in bits/param), Mano (knowledge manipulation via modular arithmetic) and Lano (hierarchical CFG structure). — [alphaXiv](https://alphaxiv.org/abs/2512.17351); [NeurIPS 2025 poster](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2025/poster/116327)
- Allen-Zhu Part 4.1 findings, as an example of a small *connectivity* change with large effects: Canon layers (residual 1-D causal convolutions with kernel size 4 linking neighboring tokens; about 0.5% more parameters) raised reasoning depth 2–4×, breadth about 30% and knowledge capacity about 10–15% in RoPE Transformers. They let NoPE match RoPE and raised GLA from 1-hop to 4-hop. The synthetic findings were then checked at 1.3B/100B real data, where Canon lifted NoPE to RoPE level and let GLA match Mamba2/GDN. — [alphaXiv](https://alphaxiv.org/abs/2512.17351)
- **Elhage et al., "Toy Models of Superposition" (Transformer Circuits Thread, 2022):** synthetic sparse features, each zero with probability S, with graded importance. A linear-bottleneck baseline is "a well understood baseline which does not exhibit superposition" and is compared against a ReLU-output model. Phase diagrams over sparsity × extra-feature importance classify regimes (not learned / superposed / dedicated dimension). Empirical diagrams are checked against closed-form losses for candidate configurations; "there really is a phase change between different strategies for encoding features." Geometry (antipodal pairs, triangles, pentagons...) is quantified with a per-feature dimensionality D_i. The authors flag that the geometry may be "idiosyncratic to the toy model." — [transformer-circuits.pub](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2022/toy_model/index.html)
- **Nanda, Chan, Lieberum, Smith, Steinhardt, "Progress measures for grokking via mechanistic interpretability" (2023 arXiv; ICLR 2023, venue: background knowledge):** fully reverse-engineered modular addition (DFT plus trig identities, "addition as rotation"). The algorithm was confirmed with weight/activation analysis and **ablations in the Fourier basis**. From this they built continuous progress measures and split training into memorization → circuit formation → cleanup. Grokking is gradual strengthening of structure plus removal of memorizing components, not a sudden jump. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.05217)
- **Liu, Kitouni, Nolte, Michaud, Tegmark, Williams, "Towards Understanding Grokking: An Effective Theory of Representation Learning" (2022 arXiv; NeurIPS 2022, venue: background knowledge):** phase diagrams over hyperparameters show four phases (comprehension, grokking, memorization, confusion). Representation learning happens only in a "Goldilocks zone" between memorization and confusion. An effective theory predicts dynamics and the dependence on training-set size. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.10343)
- **Liu, Michaud, Tegmark, "Omnigrok" (2022 arXiv; ICLR 2023, venue: background knowledge):** train vs. test loss plotted against **weight norm** form an "L" and a "U" (the LU mechanism). This explains grokking's dependence on data size and weight decay. Using it, the authors *induced* grokking on image, language and molecule tasks and *eliminated* it on algorithmic data. Initialization scale or weight norm is a control knob for when structure emerges. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.01117)
- **Kumar, Bordelon, Gershman, Pehlevan, "Grokking as the Transition from Lazy to Rich Training Dynamics" (2023 arXiv; ICLR 2024, venue: background knowledge):** delayed generalization happens when the net first fits a kernel solution with its initial features and later learns features. The conditions are misalignment between top NTK eigenvectors and labels, intermediate dataset size, and a lazy start. The feature-learning rate is set precisely by parameters that "scale the network output." Shown on polynomial regression, MNIST, 1-layer Transformers and student-teacher setups. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.06110)
- **Chizat, Oyallon, Bach, "On Lazy Training in Differentiable Programming" (2018 arXiv; NeurIPS 2019, venue: background knowledge):** lazy training comes from "a choice of scaling, often implicit," that makes the model behave like its linearization (a kernel method). It is not specific to over-parameterization, and lazy-regime CNNs perform worse. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.07956)
- **Woodworth et al., "Kernel and Rich Regimes in Overparametrized Models" (2020 arXiv; COLT 2020, venue: background knowledge):** **initialization scale** controls the kernel → rich transition and the implicit bias (RKHS-norm vs. non-RKHS). Width matters when the predictor is nonzero at init, and depth changes the transition in depth-D models. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.09277)
- **Jiang, Neyshabur, Mobahi, Krishnan, Bengio, "Fantastic Generalization Measures and Where to Find Them" (2019 arXiv; ICLR 2020, venue: background knowledge):** more than 40 complexity measures and more than 10,000 CNNs trained while varying hyperparameters systematically, with "carefully controlled experiments" to look for potentially causal links. Some popular measures failed. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.02178)
- **Dziugaite et al., "In Search of Robust Measures of Generalization" (2020 arXiv; NeurIPS 2020, venue: background knowledge):** argues that Jiang et al.'s averaged analysis can hide where measures fail. Measures "should instead be evaluated within the framework of distributional robustness", i.e. worst case across environments or hyperparameter interventions, not the average. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.11924)
- **Nguyen, Raghu, Kornblith, "Do Wide and Deep Networks Learn the Same Things?" (2020 arXiv; ICLR 2021, venue: background knowledge):** a "block structure" in representations appears when model capacity is large *relative to training set size*, linked to propagation of a dominant principal component. The block structure is model-specific, while other representations are similar across width and depth. A structural signature depends on a capacity/data control parameter, not on architecture alone. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.15327)
- **Scaling-law methodology pitfalls:** see Besiroglu et al. 2024 (wrong loss aggregation leading to early optimizer termination, implausibly narrow bootstrap CIs, rounding bias) — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.10102) — and Porian et al. 2024 (last-layer FLOP accounting, warmup length and scale-dependent tuning explain the Kaplan/Chinchilla gap) — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.19146).

### Inferences
- **Synthetic-first design:** build topology experiments on tasks with known latent structure (e.g. modular arithmetic with known Fourier features, sparse-feature toy models, compositional or graph tasks like Depo/Brevo, hierarchical CFGs) so that "emergent structure" can be scored against ground truth instead of a proxy. Then confirm a subset at a realistic scale, as Allen-Zhu does.
- **Lazy-vs-rich confound is central:** residual, parallel and composed routing change the effective output scale and depth, so they can push the network into a different lazy/rich regime at the same nominal init. A "topology changes structure" claim must show the effect holds at **matched feature-learning regime**. Sweep init scale or output multiplier α (Chizat; Woodworth; Kumar) for every topology and show the topology effect persists across α, or plot it as a 2-D phase diagram (topology × α). Monitor weight norm (Omnigrok) and kernel movement, e.g. relative change in NTK or features from init.
- **Phase diagrams over points:** the unit of evidence should be a phase boundary that *moves* with topology (e.g. the data size or weight decay at which modular/Fourier structure appears shifts for parallel vs. residual), not a single configuration where topology A shows structure and B doesn't. This mirrors Elhage's sparsity × importance and Liu's hyperparameter phase maps.
- **Interventions:** where a mechanism is hypothesized ("parallel routing forces feature specialization across branches"), test it causally with Nanda-style ablations in the hypothesized basis, branch knockout, or swapping branch outputs, instead of only observing correlational structure.
- **Robust-measure framing:** following Dziugaite et al., state the topology → structure claim as holding across a family of environments (data size, width, LR, seeds), and report the worst case, not just the average.
- **Scaling with topology as a variable:** if the program fits curves of the structural metric vs. width, depth or compute per topology, use a summed (not averaged) robust loss, multiple inits for the fit, profile-likelihood or bootstrap-over-runs CIs, and consistent FLOP accounting across topologies (parallel branches may differ in FLOPs per parameter). Besiroglu's and Porian's failure modes apply directly.

### Gaps
- The specific axes of the Liu et al. 2022 phase diagrams (e.g. decoder learning rate vs. weight decay) were not confirmed from the fetched abstract.
- No paper found that builds a *formal* causal-inference framework (e.g. DAG/do-calculus) for ML training experiments beyond the "controlled experiments / robustness across environments" framing of Jiang et al. and Dziugaite et al. The Jiang paper uses conditional-independence-style analysis, but that detail was not confirmed from the fetched abstract.
- The Kaplan et al. 2020 scaling-law paper and the original Hoffmann et al. 2022 paper were not fetched directly.

## 5. Theory-to-experiment practice: predictions, kill criteria, falsification, strong inference

### Takeaway
Use Platt's strong inference: list competing hypotheses for *why* topology would change structure (including boring ones such as an effective-LR shift, effective depth, or a lazy-regime shift), design crucial experiments whose outcomes eliminate some of them, and preregister quantitative predictions and kill criteria. Good ML examples (Omnigrok inducing and eliminating grokking, Nanda's progress measures, Schaeffer's metric predictions, Elhage's closed-form vs. empirical phase checks) all derive a prediction from a mechanism and test it in both directions.

### Cited Findings
- **Platt, "Strong Inference" (Science, 1964):** (1) devise alternative hypotheses; (2) devise a crucial experiment, or several, whose alternative outcomes each exclude one or more hypotheses; (3) run it to get a clean result; (4) recycle with sub-hypotheses. "A theory is not a theory unless it can be disproved." "The Question": "But sir, what experiment could disprove your hypothesis?" — [Platt 1964 PDF](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/philosophy/epistemology/1964-platt.pdf)
- **Omnigrok:** from the LU mechanism the authors predicted, and then showed, that grokking can be *induced* on non-algorithmic tasks and *eliminated* on algorithmic ones by controlling weight norm. This is a two-directional mechanistic test. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.01117)
- **Schaeffer et al. 2023:** stated explicit predictions from a metric-choice model (three for the GPT-3 family, two for the BIG-Bench meta-analysis), then constructed "emergence" in vision tasks to confirm the mechanism is sufficient. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.15004)
- **Nanda et al. 2023:** the reverse-engineered mechanism yielded progress measures that predict training phases. The mechanism was validated by targeted Fourier-basis ablations. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.05217)
- **Elhage et al. 2022:** empirical phase diagrams compared against closed-form losses for candidate weight configurations, a theory-vs-experiment check of where the phase boundaries fall. — [transformer-circuits.pub](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2022/toy_model/index.html)
- **Kumar et al. 2023:** the theory names conditions (NTK–label misalignment, intermediate data size, lazy start) under which grokking should appear, and the authors control it through output scale. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.06110)
- **Lipton & Steinhardt 2018:** the first troubling trend is presenting speculation as explanation, and the second is misattributing the source of gains. Both are failures that strong inference is designed to prevent. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.03341)
- **Preregistration workshop (NeurIPS 2020/2021):** protocol accepted before results, judged on design and on arguments from theory or prior evidence. — [NeurIPS 2020](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2020/workshop/16158)
- **Sculley et al. 2018:** time-stamped experiment logs limit post-hoc rationalization, and ablations should be done in isolation and in combination. — [OpenReview PDF](https://openreview.net/pdf?id=rJWF0Fywf)
- **Bell et al. 2022:** pairing preregistration with a multiverse fixes hypotheses while leaving explicit room for exploration. — [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.05985)

### Inferences
- **Competing hypotheses for "topology T changes structure S"** that the design must separate:
  - H_mech: the routing itself (information-flow constraint) causes S.
  - H_LR: T changes the effective learning rate or update size per layer (removed by per-topology LR tuning and μP-style multipliers).
  - H_regime: T shifts lazy vs. rich (removed by an α sweep and by matching kernel movement).
  - H_depth: T changes effective depth or path-length distribution (removed by matching path-length statistics or including a depth-matched control).
  - H_capacity: T changes expressivity despite equal parameter count (removed by checking that both topologies can fit the same targets, and by matched-loss comparisons).
  - H_metric: S is an artifact of the measure (removed by using several metrics with null baselines; see Q6).
  - H_noise: seed variance (removed by paired seeds and power analysis).
- **Crucial-experiment patterns:** (i) *dose–response*: if the mechanism is "parallelism forces specialization," structure should vary *monotonically* with the branching factor or degree of parallelism at fixed parameters, not just differ between two endpoints; (ii) *reversal*: an intervention that reinstates the hypothesized pathway in the baseline topology (e.g. adding a gated parallel path) should recover S, and blocking it in T should remove S, which mirrors Omnigrok's induce/eliminate logic; (iii) *mechanism-specific signature*: predict *where* (layer, branch) and *when* (training phase) S should appear, and check with ablations in the predicted basis.
- **Kill criteria (preregistered):** e.g. "If the topology effect on S is below 2 seed-std, or flips sign across the LR sweep's top-3 points, or disappears at matched training loss, H_mech is rejected for this task." Also state ahead of time which outcome would favor H_regime over H_mech (e.g. the effect vanishes once α is matched).
- **Ask Platt's Question** of each claim in the write-up: list the experiment that would have falsified it and show it was run.

### Gaps
- No ML paper found that explicitly applies Platt's strong inference as a named methodology. The ML examples above follow its logic without citing it.
- No published template of "kill criteria" for ML architecture studies was found. The criteria above are synthesized recommendations.

## 6. Comparing architectures with different inductive biases on *structure* (not accuracy): matched-loss vs. matched-step, capacity control, effect vs. a control parameter

### Takeaway
Structural comparisons need (a) a defensible alignment point: matched training loss and/or matched steps/compute, reported both ways; (b) capacity controls beyond parameter count; (c) validated structural metrics with null baselines, since CKA in particular can be manipulated and disagrees with other measures; and (d) effects plotted as a function of a control parameter (data size, width, init scale, sparsity), where they form phase diagrams or dose–response curves.

### Cited Findings
- **Ding, Denain, Steinhardt, "Grounding Representation Similarity with Statistical Testing" (NeurIPS 2021):** similarity measures should be *sensitive* to changes that affect function (probe accuracy, OOD robustness) and *specific* against changes that don't. CCA and CKA "often disagree on fundamental observations," including whether seed-only variants learn similar representations. In a benchmark of four subtasks (seeds and depth with linear probes; principal-component deletion; 100 BERT fine-tuning seeds evaluated on OOD sets such as HANS; pretraining plus fine-tuning seeds), orthogonal Procrustes was the most consistent and no metric succeeded on the hardest subtask. BERT models with near-identical in-distribution accuracy varied widely on OOD. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.01661v2); [BAIR blog](https://bair.berkeley.edu/blog/2021/11/08/similarity); [NeurIPS PDF](https://papers.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2021/file/0c0bf917c7942b5a08df71f9da626f97-Paper.pdf)
- **Davari, Horoi, Natik, Lajoie, Wolf, Belilovsky, "Reliability of CKA as a Similarity Measure in Deep Learning" (ICLR 2023):** CKA is formally sensitive to a broad class of simple transformations, which explains its outlier sensitivity, and to transformations that preserve linear separability. Representations can be changed to alter CKA substantially "without substantial functional changes." — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.16156v2); [ICLR 2023](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2023/poster/12037)
- **Nguyen, Raghu, Kornblith (ICLR 2021):** block structure appears as a function of capacity relative to dataset size, and wide vs. deep models with similar accuracy make distinct errors. Structure depends on a control parameter, and equal accuracy does not imply equal structure. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.15327)
- **Elhage et al. 2022:** structure (superposition geometry) is reported as a function of control parameters (sparsity, importance) against a linear baseline that cannot show the phenomenon. This is a positive/negative-control design. — [transformer-circuits.pub](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2022/toy_model/index.html)
- **Narang et al. 2021:** deeper models at fixed parameter count outperformed shallower ones. MoE/Switch/product-key memory helped but added parameters. Parameter count is not a sufficient capacity control, and FLOPs, depth and parameters must be reported separately. — [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2102.11972v2)
- **Kaddour et al. 2023:** comparing at a fixed *compute* budget, against a baseline whose LR schedule fully decays within that budget, erased claimed gains. The alignment point (and schedule) defines the comparison. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.06440v1)
- **Dahl et al. 2023 (AlgoPerf):** "deciding when training is complete" is one of three core challenges in comparing training algorithms. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.07179)
- **Nanda et al. 2023 / Liu et al. 2022:** structure emerges at different times (memorization → circuit formation → cleanup), so the checkpoint at which structure is measured changes the answer. — [Nanda arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.05217); [Liu arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.10343)
- **Schaeffer et al. 2023:** discontinuous metrics make smooth changes look like jumps. Prefer continuous structural scores. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.15004)
- **Musgrave et al. 2020:** random networks get high NMI on many-class data. Every metric needs a random-init or shuffled baseline. — [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2003.08505v3)

### Inferences
- **Alignment points:** report structure at (1) matched steps and tokens (same data exposure), (2) matched training loss (first checkpoint where each topology reaches loss L*, for several L*), and (3) end of a fully decayed schedule at each topology's own tuned LR. A claim that holds only under one alignment is a claim about training *speed*, not structure. Plot structure vs. training loss (a "structure–loss trajectory") per topology. If the curves overlap, topology changes the speed of reaching structure, not which structure is reached.
- **Capacity/expressivity controls beyond parameter count:** match parameters *and* report FLOPs per token, effective depth or path-length distribution, and width per path. Show both topologies can reach the same minimum training loss on the task (equal expressivity in practice). Where possible, include a "topology-scrambled" control with the same operators and parameters but random routing, to separate "any change in routing" from the specific hypothesized routing.
- **Structural metric validation:** for each metric (CKA, Procrustes, probe accuracy, modularity, Fourier-feature concentration, superposition dimensionality, effective rank), (a) compute it on random-init networks and on label-shuffled training to get null baselines; (b) measure the seed-only distribution within one topology; (c) report at least two metrics from different families (e.g. Procrustes plus CKA plus a functional probe), following Ding et al.; (d) where ground-truth structure exists (synthetic tasks), score against it directly; (e) check that metric differences track functional differences such as ablation effects or probe/OOD accuracy (Ding's sensitivity/specificity test). Treat CKA alone as insufficient (Davari et al.).
- **Effect as a function of a control parameter:** sweep at least one control parameter that the mechanism predicts should modulate the effect (data size, width, sparsity, init/output scale α, weight decay, branching factor). Report the topology effect (paired difference in structural metric, with bootstrap-over-seeds CI) as a curve or 2-D phase diagram. Elhage, Liu and Nguyen all show that structure is regime-dependent. A single-regime result invites the "cherry-picked regime" objection.

### Checklist: architecture-vs-structure experiments (synthesized from all sections)

**Design & preregistration**
1. Write down H_mech plus the competing explanations (effective LR, lazy/rich regime, effective depth, capacity, metric artifact, seed noise) and the crucial experiment that separates each (Platt 1964; Lipton & Steinhardt 2018).
2. Preregister primary structural metric(s), primary alignment point(s), primary task(s), seed count, analysis plan and kill criteria in a time-stamped log (NeurIPS preregistration workshop; Sculley et al. 2018; Bell et al. 2022).
3. Start on synthetic tasks with known ground-truth structure, then confirm on a realistic task (Allen-Zhu Part 4.1; Elhage 2022; Nanda 2023).

**Fair comparison**
4. Fix the operator set, parameter count, data stream and training budget. Report FLOPs/token, effective depth and path-length statistics per topology (Narang 2021).
5. Tune nuisance hyperparameters (LR, warmup, WD, init scale or branch multiplier) *per topology* with an equal budget and the same search method. Report isolation plots and expected-best-vs-budget curves (Tuning Playbook; Dodge 2019).
6. Show each topology's LR optimum is interior to the sweep. Report results at per-topology optima *and* at a shared LR (Bell 2022; Wortsman 2023).
7. Use fully decayed LR schedules for all arms at the given budget (Kaddour 2023).
8. Use a μP / depth-μP-style parametrization or an explicit residual/branch-multiplier sweep so topology does not silently change the effective update scale (Yang 2022; Yang 2023).
9. Match or sweep the lazy-vs-rich regime: sweep the output/init scale α, and log weight norm and feature/NTK movement from init (Chizat 2019; Woodworth 2020; Kumar 2023; Omnigrok).

**Statistics**
10. Use a paired design: the same seeds for data order, subsample and (where possible) init streams across topologies. Analyze per-seed differences (Bouthillier 2021).
11. Randomize data sampling and order, not just init. Data sampling is the largest variance source (Bouthillier 2021).
12. Seeds: at least 5 for exploration, about 10 for reported results, about 29 per arm for headline claims at P(A>B) ≥ 0.75. Run a power analysis from a pilot seed study (Bouthillier 2021; Henderson 2018; Picard 2021).
13. Treat the run as the unit of analysis. Bootstrap over runs and report mean, std, min, max and IQM plus 95% CIs (Agarwal 2021; Picard 2021).
14. Report effect sizes (P(A>B) with CI, or paired Cohen's d) and require both significant (CI_min > 0.5) and meaningful (CI_max > 0.75) (Bouthillier 2021).
15. Correct for multiple comparisons across metrics, layers, checkpoints and topologies, or confine claims to preregistered primaries (Sculley 2018).
16. Never report best-seed or minimum values. Report full distributions under a fixed budget (Lucic 2018; Picard 2021).

**Measurement**
17. Use at least two structural metrics from different families, including Procrustes or a functional probe, not CKA alone (Ding 2021; Davari 2023).
18. Give every metric null baselines (random init, shuffled labels, seed-only variation within a topology) (Musgrave 2020; Ding 2021).
19. Prefer continuous metrics to thresholded ones (Schaeffer 2023).
20. Validate that metric differences track function: ablation effects, probes, OOD behavior (Ding 2021; Nanda 2023).

**Alignment & regimes**
21. Report structure at matched steps, at matched training loss (several thresholds), and at end of training. Plot structure-vs-loss trajectories (Kaddour 2023; Dahl 2023; Nanda 2023).
22. Show the effect as a function of at least one control parameter (data size, width, sparsity, α, WD, branching factor), as a phase diagram or dose–response curve (Elhage 2022; Liu 2022; Nguyen 2021; Bell 2022).
23. Report the region of the multiverse where the claim holds and the worst case, not only the average (Bell 2022; Dziugaite 2020).

**Causal tests & robustness**
24. Run interventions: branch ablation, path knockout, reinstating the hypothesized pathway in the baseline, and ablations in the hypothesized basis. Show both induction and elimination (Omnigrok; Nanda 2023).
25. Include a random-routing control with the same operators and parameters to separate "specific topology" from "any routing change."
26. Replicate in an independent codebase or implementation and on at least one second task family (Narang 2021).
27. If fitting curves across scale, use a summed robust loss, multiple fit inits, honest CIs (bootstrap over runs) and consistent FLOP accounting (Besiroglu 2024; Porian 2024).
28. Release code, seeds, configurations, search spaces and all runs (Pineau 2021; Li & Talwalkar 2019; Dodge 2019).

### Gaps
- No paper found that systematically compares "matched-loss vs. matched-step" alignment for *representation-structure* comparisons. The recommendation to report both is a synthesis from the training-efficiency literature (Kaddour; AlgoPerf) and from phase-dependent structure (Nanda; Liu).
- Kornblith et al. 2019 ("Similarity of Neural Network Representations Revisited", the original CKA paper) was not fetched in this session, and neither were specific numbers from Ding et al. on PC-deletion sensitivity. Background knowledge says CKA is relatively insensitive to deleting low-variance principal components, but this was not verified here and is left out of the cited findings.
- No source was found on capacity/expressivity controls specific to parallel vs. serial composition at a fixed operator set. This appears to be an open methodological area that the research program would be contributing to.
